## (a) Criterion G

Setting.
- k is a subfield of P, embedded in ℝ. It may contain transcendentals.
- C/k is a smooth projective, geometrically irreducible curve of genus g. For a singular curve inside some
  V, use its normalisation; the hyperplane class pulls back to it.
- "C(ℝ) ≠ ∅" refers to this embedding.
- gon_P(C) is the least degree of a non-constant map C → ℙ¹ defined over P (equivalently, over some
  subfield of P).

### Theorem G (sufficiency) [Proof]

Suppose C(ℝ) ≠ ∅ and there is a chain of finite morphisms, all defined over some subfield k' ⊆ P,

  C = D₀ → D₁ → … → D_s,  with D_s = ℙ¹ or a k'-conic,

where each step has degree ≤ 8 or a solvable Galois closure of function fields. Then **C(P) is dense in
C(ℝ)** (Euclidean topology). In particular, gon_P(C) ≤ 8 together with C(ℝ) ≠ ∅ gives dense P-points.

*Correction to the solvable clause (Y1b, 2026-11-10).* "Solvable Galois closure" must mean the
**arithmetic** closure over k'(t), i.e. Γ = Gal(closure/k'(t)) solvable — not the geometric
monodromy. A regular extension can acquire a constant field extension in its Galois closure; then Γ
is an extension of a possibly nonsolvable constant group by the geometric monodromy. (A sufficient,
checkable condition: the closure is regular and its group is solvable.) The pencil curve of Y1b is
the counterexample to the looser reading: its geometric monodromy is the solvable (ℤ/2)ⁿ, but its
closure contains the S_n-resolvent fields of the target, so the step is not admissible for n ≥ 5.
See `Y1b.md` §4, §7.

Proof.
1. **Base case: a conic.** Let Q be a conic over k' with a real point x.
   - Pick y ∈ ℙ²(ℚ) off Q with the line yx not tangent to Q.
   - Lines through y of rational slope near yx meet Q in two real points. These are roots of a real
     quadratic over P, so they lie in P (P is closed under square roots of positive elements).
   - So Q has dense P-points, and Q ≅ ℙ¹ over P.
2. **One step.** Let f: D → D' with D'(P) dense in D'(ℝ). Take x ∈ D(ℝ) and a neighbourhood U of x.
   - f is non-constant on the real arc through x, because that arc is Zariski-dense in D. So
     f(U ∩ D(ℝ)) contains an open interval of D'(ℝ).
   - Choose t ∈ D'(P) in that interval, not a branch point, and y ∈ U ∩ D(ℝ) with f(y) = t.
   - The residue field E of y is an extension of P of degree ≤ deg f, and y gives a real embedding of E.
   - If deg f ≤ 8: E = P(θ), with θ a real root of an irreducible polynomial of degree ≤ 8 over P. So
     θ ∈ P and y is a P-point.
   - If the step is solvable: the decomposition group at t is a subgroup of the monodromy group, which is
     solvable. So E has solvable Galois closure over P, and its real embedding lands in P by the tower
     lemma (X1 §2.2). Z8 notes this lemma holds over any subfield of P.
3. Induct down the chain. QED.

Remarks.
- The PLAN's version of (G) also asks for "a real point in the fibre over some k'-point". That is automatic
  once C(ℝ) ≠ ∅, by step 2. C(ℝ) ≠ ∅ is necessary anyway, so it is the only real condition.
- Theorem G is sufficient only. P-points can also arise from non-algebraic P-operations.

### Sources of P-maps of degree ≤ 8 [Proof unless marked]

- **(G0) g = 0.** C is a conic; use step 1.
- **(G1) Embedded curves.** Suppose C ⊂ ℙ^N over k' has degree e ≤ 8.
  - Project from a k'-rational codimension-2 linear space that misses C. This gives a map of degree e.
  - Such spaces form a dense open subset of a Grassmannian. The Grassmannian is rational, so k'-points
    exist.
- **(G2) Hyperelliptic, any g ≥ 2.** The canonical map is 2:1 onto a k-form of ℙ¹, i.e. a conic, which has
  a real point. Degree 2.
- **(G3) 2 ≤ g ≤ 5.** The canonical model has degree 2g − 2 ≤ 8; apply (G1).
- **(G4) Index form.** Suppose C has a P-rational divisor D, not necessarily effective, of degree d with
  g + 1 ≤ d ≤ 8.
  - By Riemann–Roch, h⁰(D) ≥ d − g + 1 ≥ 2. L(D) is a P-vector space, so |D| contains a P-rational pencil
    of degree ≤ d. This needs g ≤ 7.
  - P-divisors come from:
    - K, of degree 2g − 2;
    - the hyperplane class H, of degree e;
    - fibres of P-maps;
    - P-points.
  - Let I be the gcd of the degrees available. Then:
    - **g = 6** works if I | 7 or I | 8. For C ⊂ ℙ^N of degree e, I | gcd(e, 10), so this holds whenever
      **5 ∤ e**.
    - **g = 7** works if I | 8, which holds whenever **3 ∤ e**.
- **(G5) One P-point gives dense P-points for every g ≤ 8.**
  - Let p be a P-point of a non-hyperelliptic C. Project the canonical curve from the osculating
    (g−3)-plane at p, which is P-rational.
  - The moving part has degree 2g − 2 − a_{g−2}(p) ≤ g, because a_{g−2}(p) ≥ g − 2. So the degree is
    ≤ g ≤ 8.
  - A P-divisor of degree 1, with no P-point, gives only (G4), i.e. g ≤ 7.
  - Projecting from more general P-points never gets below degree g. Lower degree needs special
    (Brill–Noether) divisors.
- **(G6) Galois-stable pencils.** Suppose W¹_d(C), with d ≤ 8 and viewed as a k-scheme, has a closed point L
  with h⁰(L) = 2 whose residue field has a real embedding landing in P (for example: degree ≤ 8 over k, a
  tower of steps of degree ≤ 8, or solvable).
  - Then L gives a map C → C_L over P, where C_L is a conic.
  - C_L has a real point, the image of a point of C(ℝ). So C_L ≅ ℙ¹ over P, which gives a P-map of degree
    ≤ d.
  - Cases where this applies:
    - **Unique g¹_d:** trigonal curves with g ≥ 5, and any g¹_d with g > (d−1)² (Castelnuovo–Severi).
    - **Smooth plane curves of degree m:** the g²_m is unique, so the Brauer–Severi surface has index 1
      or 3. A cubic splitting field always has a real embedding, of degree 3 ≤ 8. So C is a plane curve
      over P. For m ≤ 9, project from a P-point of C.
    - **General genus 6:** W¹_4 is finite of length 5 (the Castelnuovo count). Complex conjugation acts on
      a scheme of odd length, so it fixes some point. That point's residue field has degree ≤ 5, so it
      embeds in P. If W¹_4 is infinite, Mumford's theorem says C is trigonal, a plane quintic or
      bielliptic. The first two are handled above.
    - **Bielliptic, g ≥ 6:** the involution is unique (Castelnuovo–Severi), so there is a map C → E over
      k of degree 2.
      - Pushforward of P-divisors gives P-divisors on E of the same degree.
      - If E gets one of degree in [2, 8], the tower C → E → ℙ¹ works. For example, when e is an odd
        multiple of 5, some combination aH + bK has degree 5, and its pushforward has degree 5.
      - Otherwise this case is [Open].
  - **Parity trick only at g = 2, 6, 14 [Proof of the arithmetic].** At ρ = 0 (g = 2k, d = k + 1) the
    number of g¹_d is Catalan(k): 1, 2, 5, 14, 42, 132 for g = 2, …, 12. This is odd only for g = 2, 6, 14.
    At g = 14 there are 429 pencils, so the residue degree is not small.
  - **g = 8:** there are 14 pencils g¹_5, and possibly no real one.
    - Eisenbud–Harris: the monodromy on such sets is the full symmetric group. So a real pencil on a very
      general curve has residue degree 14, with point stabiliser S₁₃ maximal of index 14. This is not
      reachable by towers [Proof sketch for very general curves; Heuristic for specific ones].
- **(G7) Positive-dimensional W¹_d.** This needs a P-point on W¹_d ∖ W²_d, which is the same problem one level
  up (W¹_d is itself a k-variety). Nothing here is unconditional. For a general curve of genus 7, W¹_5 is a
  curve [Open].

### Criterion G, clean statement

Let C/k with k ⊆ P, C(ℝ) ≠ ∅, genus g. Let I be the gcd of the degrees of the known P-rational divisors;
it divides 2g − 2, and divides e if C ⊂ ℙ^N has degree e.

- **[Proof] Dense P-points hold unconditionally** if any of the following holds:
  - g = 0, or 2 ≤ g ≤ 5;
  - C is hyperelliptic or trigonal (any g);
  - C is embedded over k' of degree e ≤ 8;
  - g = 6, and 5 ∤ I, or W¹_4 is finite, or C is a plane quintic;
  - g = 7 and I | 8;
  - g = 1 and C has a P-divisor of degree in [2, 8];
  - g ≤ 8 and C has one P-point.
  More generally: whenever there is a chain of P-maps of degree ≤ 8 or solvable steps ending at a conic.
- **Residual cases [Open]:**
  - g = 1 with P-index > 8. Whether a real genus-1 curve can keep index > 8 over P is itself open.
  - g = 6 bielliptic, with every P-divisor degree divisible by 10.
  - g = 7 with 3 | I, no P-point, and no P-rational special pencil.
  - g ≥ 8 with no P-point and no P-rational g¹_{≤8}.
    - Brill–Noether gives geometric gonality ⌊(g+3)/2⌋ ≤ 8 for g ≤ 13, but nothing makes that pencil
      P-rational.
    - For ρ > 0 one needs a P-point on W¹_d, which is recursive. For ρ = 0 the Galois orbit is generically
      large.
  - g ≥ 14: geometric gonality may exceed 8, so only special curves qualify.
- **Correction to the PLAN:** "genus ≤ 13 suffices" is **not** a theorem. The genus at which density is
  unconditional is **≤ 5** in general (≤ 7 with index conditions, ≤ 8 with a P-point).

## (b) The open window on V_τ (n = 9)

Setting. C ⊂ V_τ ⊂ ℙ⁸ is an irreducible curve, C ≠ ℓ_τ, defined over L ⊆ P, with H = Gal ⊇ A₉ (a
primitive group whose heart is irreducible). Z8 extends Z5 to every subfield of P. C has geometric genus g,
degree e, and spans ℙ^r (span is L-rational).

### Constraints used
1. **Z5:** e ≤ 4g − 4, and g ≥ 2.
2. **Z8:** every L-rational quadric of V_τ's six-dimensional system is smooth of rank 9. So the six quadrics
   restrict to six *independent* quadrics on the span ℙ^r once r ≥ 4. Also r ≥ 3, and r = 3 forces e ≤ 4.
3. **Castelnuovo.** g ≤ π(e, r), where m = ⌊(e−1)/(r−1)⌋, ε = e − 1 − m(r−1) and
   π = m(m−1)(r−1)/2 + mε. This holds for geometric genus too.
4. **Embedding dimension.** r ≤ h⁰(H|_C) − 1. That gives r ≤ e − g if e > 2g − 2; r ≤ g − 1 if e = 2g − 2;
   and r ≤ ⌊e/2⌋ (Clifford) otherwise.
5. **New: quadric count [Proof].**
   - Let Γ = C ∩ (general hyperplane), a set of e points in uniform position in ℙ^{r−1}.
   - From 0 → I_C(1) → I_C(2) → I_{Γ,ℙ^{r−1}}(2): h⁰(I_C(2)) ≤ C(r+1, 2) − h_Γ(2).
   - Castelnuovo: h_Γ(2) ≥ min(e, 2r − 1).
   - Six independent quadrics are needed, so C(r+1, 2) − min(e, 2r−1) ≥ 6.
   - **r = 4** gives h⁰(I_C(2)) ≤ 10 − min(e, 7). That is < 6 once e ≥ 5, and Z8 already excludes e ≤ 4.
     **So r = 4 is excluded entirely**, which sharpens Z8.
   - r = 5 gives h⁰(I_C(2)) ≤ 15 − min(e, 9) = 6 for e ≥ 9. So equality is needed.
6. **r = 5, e ≥ 11 is excluded [Proof sketch].**
   - Equality in (5) means Γ (e ≥ 11 = 2(r−1)+3 points in ℙ⁴) imposes only 9 conditions on quadrics.
   - Castelnuovo's lemma then puts Γ on a rational normal quartic, so C lies on a surface S ⊂ ℙ⁵ of minimal
     degree 4 (a scroll, a cone, or the Veronese surface).
   - h⁰(I_S(2)) = 6, and I_S ⊆ I_C, so the six V_τ quadrics span H⁰(I_S(2)).
   - The ideal of S is generated by these quadrics, so V_τ ∩ ℙ⁵ = S. Then S would be a surface inside the
     irreducible surface V_τ of degree 64. Contradiction.
7. **(g, e) = (6, 10) is excluded [Proof sketch].**
   - Here e = 2g − 2 with r = 5 forces H|_C = K, i.e. C is a canonical genus-6 curve, which lies on exactly
     21 − 15 = 6 quadrics.
   - By Petri/Enriques–Babbage, those quadrics cut out a surface: a del Pezzo quintic, a cubic scroll, or the
     Veronese surface.
   - That surface would lie in V_τ ∩ ℙ⁵. Same contradiction as in 6.

Wolfram input [Wolfram] (constraints 1, 3, 4, 5, 6; r ∈ {5, …, 8}; e ≥ 8; output = (g, e, admissible r)):
```
castel[e_, r_] := Module[{m = Floor[(e-1)/(r-1)], eps}, eps = e-1-m (r-1); m (m-1) (r-1)/2 + m eps];
rmax[g_, e_] := Which[e > 2g-2, e-g, e == 2g-2, g-1, True, Floor[e/2]];
quadOK[e_, r_] := (Binomial[r+1, 2] - Min[e, 2r-1]) >= 6;
ok[g_, e_, r_] := r <= e && r <= rmax[g, e] && g <= castel[e, r] && quadOK[e, r] && !(r == 5 && e >= 11);
Table[{g, e, Select[Range[5, 8], ok[g, e, #] &]}, {g, 3, 13},
  {e, Select[Range[8, 4g-4], Function[e, AnyTrue[Range[5, 8], ok[g, e, #] &]]]}]
```

  g = 13: e = 15–48.
- Lower edge: Castelnuovo at r = 8 (or r = 6). Upper edge: Z5's e ≤ 4g − 4.

### Window that Criterion G turns into P-points

Assume also C(ℝ) ≠ ∅. Theorem R's nondegeneracy (points off ℓ_τ etc.) follows from density. Here
I | gcd(e, 2g−2).

**Class A — unconditional [Proof, given (a)]: 28 pairs.**
- (3, 8): genus-3 octic spanning ℙ⁵.
- g = 4: e = 9, 10, 11, 12.
- g = 5: e = 10, …, 16.
- g = 6: e = 12, 13, 14, 16, 17, 18, 19 (5 ∤ e, by (G4)).
- g = 6: e = 15. Done by (G6): W¹_4 finite gives parity; trigonal and plane quintic are covered; bielliptic
  is covered because a combination aH + bK has degree 5, which pushes forward to E.
- g = 7: e = 13, 14, 16, 17, 19, 20, 22, 23 (3 ∤ e, by (G4)).

**Class B — P-points only under extra structure [Open].**
- (6, 20): fine unless C is bielliptic.
- g = 7, e ∈ {12, 15, 18, 21, 24}: needs C trigonal or hyperelliptic, a P-rational g¹_{5,6,7}, or one P-point.
- g = 8, e = 13–28: needs one P-point (then (G5)) or a P-rational g¹_{≤8}.
- 9 ≤ g ≤ 13 (ranges above): needs a P-rational g¹_{≤8}. Geometric gonality ≤ 8 here; P-rationality is the
  problem.
- g ≥ 14: only special curves.

**Recommendation for B1.** Hunt in Class A, in this order:
1. (3, 8, ℙ⁵): Z8's survivor; projectively normal; cheap numerics.
2. g = 4, e = 9–12 and g = 5, e = 10–16.
3. g = 6, e ∈ {12, …, 19}, and g = 7 with 3 ∤ e.

Heuristic. Every Class A curve has C² = 2g − 2 − 3e ≤ −20 on V_τ (K_V = O(3)), so it is rigid and isolated.
Z8's expected dimension g + 5 − 3e < 0 (τ free) holds throughout Class A. Any hit would be special, e.g. a
curve with nontrivial deck stabiliser over a subfield [Heuristic, weak].

## (c) GAP / Wolfram check of Z7's Dixon–Mortimer step

GAP is not installed on this machine, so I used Wolfram's group functions plus a maximal-subgroup argument.
A transitive action of degree m is the same thing as a subgroup of index m. Index depends only on the
order, so iso-type subgroup lists are enough to read off the indices.

### n ≤ 8 [Wolfram]

Input:
```
Union[ord/(FiniteGroupData[#,"Order"]& /@ FiniteGroupData[G,"Subgroups"])]
```
then select the indices < C(n, 2). Results:

| G | indices < C(n,2) | exceptions (not 1, 2, n, 2n) |
|---|---|---|
| A₅ | 1, 5, 6 | 6 (D₁₀, i.e. PSL(2,5) ≅ A₅ on 6 points) |
| S₅ | 1, 2, 5, 6 | 6 (F₂₀ = AGL(1,5), i.e. PGL(2,5) on 6 points) |
| A₆ | 1, 6, 10 | 6 (twin A₅), 10 (3²:4) |
| S₆ | 1, 2, 6, 10, 12 | 6 (twin S₅), 10 (S₃≀S₂, bisections); 12 also has a twin class |
| A₇ | 1, 7, 15 | **15 (PSL(2,7), two classes)** |
| A₈ | 1, 8, 15 | 15 (AGL(3,2) ≅ … ⊂ A₈ ≅ GL(4,2), two classes) |

FiniteGroupData has no subgroup list for S₇ and S₈, so I argued directly [Proof + Wolfram]:
- If H ≤ S_n has index < C(n,2), then H ∩ A_n has index < C(n,2) in A_n. So it is A_n, A_{n−1}, or the
  15-index group.
- [Wolfram] **No odd permutation normalises PSL(2,7) ⊂ A₇, nor AGL(3,2) ⊂ A₈.** This was a brute-force
  test over all of S₇ and S₈. The groups have orders 168 and 1344 and even generators. Input as in the log:
  `Count[GroupElements[SymmetricGroup[n]], g_ /; Signature[PermutationList[g,n]]==-1 && AllTrue[gens, MemberQ[hel, PermutationProduct[InversePermutation[g],#,g]]&]]`
  gives 0 in both cases.
- So those subgroups have index 30 in S_n, which is ≥ C(7,2) = 21 and ≥ C(8,2) = 28.
- Result: **S₇: {1, 2, 7, 14}; S₈: {1, 2, 8, 16}. Neither has exceptions.**

### n ≥ 9 [Proof sketch, citing the classification]

Take H ≤ G ∈ {S_n, A_n} of index < C(n,2), and a maximal subgroup M ⊇ H. M also has index < C(n,2). By the
O'Nan–Scott / Liebeck–Praeger–Saxl classification, M is one of:
- **intransitive, k ≥ 2:** index C(n,k) ≥ C(n,2). Excluded.
- **imprimitive S_a ≀ S_b:** index n!/(a!^b b!). [Wolfram] This is ≥ C(n,2) for every composite n in
  7…200; it fails only at n = 6. The general case follows from the n = 2a, b = 2 term, which is
  C(n, n/2)/2.
- **primitive, not containing A_n:** order < 3^n (Maróti 2002). Then the index is
  > (n!/2)/3^n ≥ C(n,2) for n ≥ 11 [Wolfram: the inequality fails only at n = 9, 10 in 9…200].
  - n = 9: the largest such groups are PΓL(2,8) (1512, index 120 in A₉) and AGL(2,3) (432).
  - n = 10: the largest is PΓL(2,9) (1440, index ≥ 1260).
  - These sizes come from the primitive-group tables [Citation].
- So M = A_n (when G = S_n), or M = G_1 (point stabiliser, index n).
  - If M = A_n, recurse.
  - If M = G_1, then |G_1 : H| < (n−1)/2 < n−1. By Thm 5.2A (Jordan: index < m in S_m/A_m, m ≥ 5, forces
    A_m; consistent with the m = 5…8 data above), H ⊇ A_{n−1}.
- **Conclusion:** S_n has transitive actions of degree 1, 2, n, 2n below C(n,2), and A_n has 1, n.
- n = 9 directly from the ATLAS maximal subgroups:
  - A₉: A₈ (9), then S₇ (36);
  - S₉: A₉, S₈ (9), then S₇×S₂ (36).

### Verdict on Z7

- **Z7's conclusion stands** for n ≥ 9: m ∈ {n, 2n} ∪ [C(n,2), ∞).
- Z7's small-n list is correct except **n = 7**. It says "none below 14", but **A₇ has two classes of
  degree-15 actions** (PSL(2,7)), and 15 < 21. S₇ indeed has none.
- Two further additions:
  - n = 6 also has the twin index-12 class;
  - n = 5 includes A₅ on 6 points.
- This affects only the small-n readout tables, not the n ≥ 9 claim.
